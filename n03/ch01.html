Chapter One: The Weight of Steel

A midnight train, a bitter betrayal, and a sister torn from her last remaining anchor. When a university raid shatters her world in the capital, twenty-year-old Sarah flees through rain-slicked alleys with her brother, Liam. But as the iron doors of the midnight transport seal, Liam makes a devastating choice—pushing Sarah onto the moving train while he stays behind to confront the ghosts of their family’s past. Alone, stateless, and shivering in an oversized coat that still smells of dormitory cordite, Sarah steps off into the freezing mud of a nameless frontier town, forced to survive on sheer, desperate resolve.

The cold did not bite; it paralyzed.

By the time the secondary transport dropped Sarah off at the edge of the frontier jurisdiction, the diesel engine was coughing black phlegm into the frost-choked air before rattling back down the unpaved highway, leaving her alone with the dark.

She stood motionless beneath the flickering amber glow of a single municipal streetlight, her fingers locked inside the pockets of an oversized wool coat that still smelled faintly of cordite, cheap solvent, and old dormitory dust. Her legs felt hollow, like dry reeds ready to snap under the slightest pressure. Every breath burned her throat, tasting of iron and ash.

Two years after Althea Plaza had turned their world into a graveyard, the fire had finally caught up to them. Or rather, it had been handed over.

Marcus.

The name tasted like copper in her mouth. Three years of shared blueprints, shared midnight debates over structural resistance points, and late-night study sessions in the Cranston University annex—all traded away for a clean record and a provisional clearance pass. She could still hear the distinct, heavy thud of the security wedges hitting the corridor floor outside Dormitory 4B. They hadn't even finished scoring the glass bottlenecks for the second crate of Molotovs.

“Grab the pack, Sarah. Now!”

Liam’s voice cut through the memory, sharp and absolute, overriding the paralyzing panic that had threatened to glue her shoes to the linoleum floor. They had bolted through the service fire escape just as the reinforced timber doors splintered inward under the weight of the tactical team's hydraulic rams. Glass bottles had shattered against the concrete behind them, spilling flammable fuel into the stairwell in a volatile slick that almost cost them their footing.

They had run blind through the rain-slicked alleys of the industrial district, their lungs burning, lungs screaming for oxygen that refused to fill them. The city had felt like a cage closing its bars one by one.

And then, the station.

The midnight commuter service to the western sectors was idling against the platform, its industrial exhaust hissing into the fog. The air inside the carriage had been thick with the smell of wet wool and stale tobacco, a sanctuary of indifference. Sarah had collapsed onto the wooden bench, her hands shaking so violently she couldn't zip her jacket.

Then came the hiss of the pneumatic doors beginning to seal.

Liam hadn't sat down. He had lingered by the threshold, his face pale under the flickering platform lights, his eyes fixed not on the empty seats inside, but back toward the glowing skyline of the capital where their parents' names still sat unceremoniously buried under the state tribunal’s closed dockets.

Just as the rubber seals met with a heavy thud, Liam had shoved her back into the aisle with a force that knocked the wind from her ribs.

“Go,” he had shouted over the rising whine of the turbine engines, his fingers gripping the exterior handrail. “Get to the border. Find a way across to West Erden. Don't look back, Sarah. I’m staying to finish this for Mom and Dad.”

“Liam, no—!”

She had lunged forward, slamming her palms against the reinforced glass, screaming his name as the train jerked violently forward, wrenching him away from the doorframe and leaving him standing alone on the dark concrete platform, a solitary silhouette swallowed by the steam and the gathering shadows.

A sudden gust of wind howling down the narrow alleyway snapped Sarah back to the present.

The border town was nameless to her—a cluster of slate-roofed cottages and corrugated tin warehouses clinging desperately to the edge of the neutral zone like barnacles on a hull. Windows were shuttered tight against the midnight curfew, slivers of pale lamplight leaking through the cracks. In the distance, the low, rhythmic thrum of automated patrol drones vibrated through the damp soil.

Sarah pulled the heavy canvas strap of her rucksack higher onto her shoulder. Her muscles ached with a profound, bone-deep exhaustion, but beneath the fatigue, something else was stirring—a cold, hard knot of resolve that felt dangerously like survival.

She took her first step into the unpaved mud of the village street, leaving the footprints of a ghost behind her, and walked straight into the dark.
