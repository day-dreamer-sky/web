Chapter Two: Salt and Ash

In a town built on salt and silence, a false name is the only armor left. Freezing beneath the damp stone arches of Saltmere’s coastal drainage bridge, Sarah stumbles upon The Gull’s Rest—a slate-roofed inn reeking of sizzling bacon, fresh bread, and home. Adopting the name “Clara,” she trades relentless, bone-breaking labor for a cot in a rat-infested attic room dumped with junk. Between scrubbing grease alongside Martha, the severe innkeeper, and dodging the cynical, sleep-deprived glare of her son Tony, Sarah buries herself in relentless work—knowing that if she pauses for even a second, the crushing weight of her grief will destroy her.

The wind off the salt flats didn't just bite; it stripped the warmth straight from your bones.

By the time Sarah reached the border town of Saltmere, the night had swallowed whatever was left of her resolve. There were no welcoming lights, no open doors for travelers without papers or coin. Shivering violently, she had curled into herself beneath the concrete arch of an old drainage bridge, pressing her back against the damp stone. Every breath came out in ragged plumes of white mist. She spent the hours listening to the rhythmic, mocking slap of the tide against the pylons, clutching Liam’s oversized coat around her like a shield against a world that had already broken her.

When dawn finally dragged a sickly, gray light across the horizon, she staggered toward the coast. There, tucked between two weathered salt-houses, sat a modest, slate-roofed inn bearing a faded wooden sign: The Gull’s Rest.

Inside, the air was thick with the rich, heavy scent of baking bread, sizzling bacon, and roasting coffee. It smelled like a home she no longer had.

Sarah stepped up to the front desk, her fingers buried deep in her pockets to hide their trembling. The woman behind the counter—mid-forties, with sharp, intelligent eyes, flour-dusted apron strings, and hair pulled back in a practical knot—was moving with frantic, mechanical precision, balancing a stack of ledger books while shouting an order toward the kitchen.

"Excuse me," Sarah said, her voice dry and raspy. "I... I need the cheapest room you have."

The woman—Martha Brown—stopped, turning to appraise Sarah with a look that took in the soot-smudged cheeks, the oversized coat, and the hollow exhaustion in her eyes.

"A single room with a cot is three silver crowns a night," Martha said flatly, her tone hardened by years of border-town survival. "In advance."

Sarah’s throat tightened. She emptied her pockets onto the counter: a handful of copper coins and a rusted train token. Not even enough for a loaf of bread, let alone a bed.

Before Martha could dismiss her, Sarah noticed the sweat beading on the innkeeper's forehead, the way her eyes kept darting toward the crowded dining room where patrons were banging silverware on tables.

"I wonder if you have any job offer here," Sarah said quickly, her desperation cutting through her pride. "I saw you quite busy in this morning session."

Martha paused, studying her with a sharp curiosity. "We do need extra hands for cooking and housekeeping. What do you know about that?"

"I am a great cook," Sarah answered, her voice steadying with a sudden surge of survival instinct. "I can cook breakfast very fast. I can do cleaning and laundering quickly."

Martha leaned against the counter, her gaze unblinking. "You have no place to stay now, do you?"

Sarah shook her head slowly.

Martha stared at her for a long, measuring beat, as if weighing the risk against the sheer mountain of work ahead of her. "I am a very practical person and won’t ask questions from a runaway girl. Behave yourself and do not make trouble. You may stay in my attic room, and then we discuss your salary after reviewing your performance."

Sarah let out a breath she didn't realize she’d been holding. "Fine. I won’t disappoint you."

"Name?"

"Clara," Sarah said, the lie tasting bitter on her tongue.

Martha turned her head toward the swinging kitchen doors and shouted, "Tony! We have extra hands now. Lead this girl to our attic."

A moment later, a young man with messy hair and an apron slung haphazardly over one shoulder pushed through the doors, wiping his hands on a rag. He took one look at Sarah, then at his mother. "Mom. Are you serious?"

"We have lots of guests coming in today. I need her to start housekeeping now. Move!"

Grumbling under his breath, Tony motioned for Sarah to follow him up a narrow, creaking set of stairs that smelled of dust and old timber. He threw open a low wooden door at the top of the landing.

"I don’t believe my mom let you stay here," Tony muttered, gesturing to a cramped, shadowy space crammed with discarded furniture, broken crates, and thick cobwebs. "We use this attic for dumping junk. Now use your creativity to make your bed out of these junks and enjoy dancing with rats at night."

Sarah bit her lip, tasting copper. "No problem. I've handled worse."

Tony raised an eyebrow, a flicker of grudging respect crossing his features. "Be my guest. Meet you downstairs in ten minutes. Show us how good you can cook."

The rest of the day was a blur of searing heat, scalding water, and relentless motion. Sarah kept herself fiercely, desperately occupied—scrubbing floors, hauling linens, flipping eggs at the stove until her arms felt like lead. She didn't allow herself a single moment to stop, knowing that if she paused for even a second, the silence would rush in and crush her.

By midnight, the inn finally fell quiet.

Sarah climbed back up to the attic. There was no bed, only a worn-out, stained sleeping mat laid directly over the rough floorboards, covered by a moth-eaten, broken blanket.

She lay down, shivering as the chill of the coastal night bled through the wooden roof tiles. Staring up into the darkness, she watched a thin sliver of pale moonlight stretch across the dusty floor.

The quiet was absolute. And in that quiet, the armor cracked.

She closed her eyes, and suddenly she wasn't in a drafty, rat-infested attic. She was back home, years ago, before the tribunals and the occupation—before her parents were taken. She felt the phantom weight of her father's arms lifting her high into the air, the bright spring sun warming her face, the sound of her own innocent laughter echoing across a green park as paper kites danced in the wind.

A sharp, physical ache bloomed in her chest. She turned onto her side, burying her face into the rough fabric of her sleeve. She tried to swallow the sob, biting down hard against her wrist, but it was no use.

Hot, silent tears carved burning tracks down her cheeks, soaking into the stained mat as the sea wind howled outside her window, reminding her that she was entirely alone in the dark.
